The journey

Both my parents work in journalism. My father still talks about what it costs to keep a publication alive: the deadlines, the price of paper, the duty to print something risky anyway. I absorbed the idea that a word has a consequence before I understood what a word was.

Theatre came next, and elocution competitions, which is where I learned that an audience decides about you in the first ten seconds and spends the rest of the time confirming it.

Four years of direction and editing at Annapurna College of Film and Media in Hyderabad. I shot and cut my own work, and I still do, which is why I can make a film rather than brief one.

Film gave me the habit that runs through everything after it: a story is an argument with a running time, and the order in which someone receives the facts decides which ones they believe.

My diploma film Dubki travelled to festivals in ten countries and is being dubbed into German for schools and hospitals across Europe. My documentary Musi: The Fading River was an official selection at IFF Ekotopfilm and Envirofilm in Slovakia, the oldest environmental film festival in the world.

I arrived at Oho Gujarati as its third employee and left as Head of Creative Development, owning development, production oversight and the editorial line for everything the platform released. Before that, a year of development, direction and advertising work at CineMan Productions, one of the prominent production houses in Indian cinema.

Deciding what gets made is the most useful training I have had for positioning a company. You price an idea before an audience has confirmed it, and then you find out in public whether you were right.

25 original series developed. A content team grown from three people to more than fifteen, all of whom I hired, briefed and reviewed. In April 2026 the whole catalogue, more than thirty originals, was acquired by Shemaroo Entertainment, a listed entertainment group.

The channel is Navi Savar: developmental journalism, solutions reporting and long interviews, in a market where outrage usually takes the attention. I set the editorial strategy and built the weekly process a small team could repeat without me in the room.

I wrote titles and rebuilt openings every week for ten months, and the numbers told me which version people stayed for. Most writing advice is guesswork. That year I got to check.

650+ videos published inside ten months, with a small team and zero spend on advertising. Most of what I know about the first ninety seconds of anything comes from these months.

I landed in the United States in August 2024 for a master's in advertising at the Savannah College of Art and Design, where I went deep on generative systems and narrative. Living near a technology campus settled the question of what I wanted to write about.

I worked through the degree. A Deloitte internship through SCADpro, where I led the team. An internship at Nexerios on brand strategy, including the investor deck they used to move their fundraising forward.

I led a team of five on a United States Postal Service project, presented the work to the client, and they accepted our strategy and messaging recommendations.

Principles

My school was founded on Gandhian principles, so I spent my childhood inside the idea that you move a person by taking their position seriously. At home my father kept putting on Chaplin, who could hold a room for eighty minutes and still say something true about being poor.

Those two sat side by side in my head and became how I understand communication, and society with it. Reach the heart, and earn the attention while you do it. Both men did it at a scale nobody has matched since, with very little to work with.

Work that picks one and surrenders the other is everywhere. Serious work turns worthy and goes unwatched; entertaining work travels fast and says little. The combination is the standard I hold my own work to, and the reason I would rather build something people join than something they merely receive.

The other thing I hold to is less romantic. Most of the people who decide about a company will never use the product: the investor, the candidate, the journalist, the regulator, the buyer who reads two paragraphs and leaves. They meet the sentences instead, and they judge the company on those. So I treat the writing as part of the product, and I hold it to the same standard of accuracy the product is held to.

Everything I do answers one question: what is this, and why should anybody care. A museum panel answers it in eight seconds for a stranger holding a ticket. A pitch deck answers it in twenty minutes for someone holding a cheque. A launch film answers it in ninety seconds for a person who came for something else. Same question, different patience.

That is why the formats look varied and the practice is narrow. I have built the story and the thing that carries it often enough to know which format a message deserves, and I arrive with a method rather than a house style to impose on you.

Film

I write, direct and cut my own films, and two of them have travelled a good deal further than the school they were made in.

Between them they have reached festivals in ten countries, a German dub for European schools and hospitals, a university syllabus in the United States, and an invitation to Slovakia. All of which taught me more about an audience than any brief has.

Sixteen minutes, made as my graduation film. An eight-year-old boy living in a city slum walks to a bungalow nearby and discovers it has a swimming pool. What the water gives him, the class line takes back. I wrote and directed it, and it premiered in January 2021.

It has since screened at festivals in ten countries, including Ukraine, the United Kingdom, Italy, Uruguay, Germany, Austria, Hungary, the United States, South Korea and India, among them the 16th Busan International Kids and Youth Film Festival in Korea, and SCHLiNGEL, the international festival for children and young audiences, in Germany. It carries a festival nomination on IMDb.

It is now being dubbed into German and shown in schools and hospitals across Europe, and it is taught inside a university syllabus in the United States. A student film that ended up in classrooms is the recognition I value most.

Seven minutes on the Musi in Hyderabad, told by the people living along its banks. The river flooded a city in 1908; today it is a trickle. I directed, produced and shot it.

Official selection at IFF Ekotopfilm and Envirofilm in Slovakia, the oldest environmental film festival in the world, with a forty-five year history. The festival invited me out to Slovakia for it.

Building

Four projects I keep running alongside the client work.

A community for filmmakers working with AI, a typography archive that became a research corpus, a product in build, and a benchmark for image models.

A site for people making films with AI who care about the film itself. We curate work, write about workflows, and ask people how they actually made the thing. It started because the careful work was getting buried under demos.

I founded it to research and document the typography of a single city, recording each painter's name alongside their board, which design archives rarely do. It grew into a community of graphic designers, researchers, type nerds and the painters themselves, and it runs as a nonprofit.

It is now the reference corpus underneath the benchmark, so lettering that was treated as anonymous has become the standard a frontier model gets measured against. Founding something and keeping it alive for three years taught me more about audience than any campaign has.

A platform where a crew works together and real actors upload their own performances, while generated environments carry the rest. Every creative decision stays with a person. With two co-founders. I handle the narrative, the product story and the workflow research.

Ask an image model for a shop sign in English and it gives you dozens of typographic styles. Ask for the same sign in a non-Latin script and the letterforms come back broken, in a single style. Evaluations report both as the same capability, because the people writing them read English.

The benchmark makes the difference countable. Readers of the script grade legibility, correctness of the letterforms and the range of styles produced, measured against a corpus of real hand-painted lettering recorded with the painter's name and the year. The rubric is prose and I am on its fourth rewrite, because every ambiguity in the wording becomes noise in the results.

I am looking for researchers working on instruction alignment and controllability in diffusion models, since the benchmark gets more useful in their hands than in mine.
