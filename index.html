The long answer

Where my thinking comes from

A Gandhian school, and the Chaplin films my father kept putting on.

My school was founded on Gandhian principles, so I spent my childhood inside the idea that you move a person by taking their position seriously. At home my father kept putting on Chaplin, who could hold a room for eighty minutes and still say something true about being poor.

Those two sat side by side in my head and became how I understand communication, and society with it. Reach the heart, and earn the attention while you do it. Both men did it at a scale nobody has matched since, with very little to work with.

Work that picks one and surrenders the other is everywhere. Serious work turns worthy and goes unwatched; entertaining work travels fast and says little. The combination is the standard I hold my own work to, and the reason I would rather build something people join than something they merely receive.

The other thing I hold to is less romantic. Most of the people who decide about a company will never use the product: the investor, the candidate, the journalist, the regulator, the buyer who reads two paragraphs and leaves. They meet the sentences instead, and they judge the company on those. So I treat the writing as part of the product, and I hold it to the same standard of accuracy the product is held to.

How the range works

The formats change because the reader changes. The question stays the same.

Everything I do answers one question: what is this, and why should anybody care. A museum panel answers it in eight seconds for a stranger holding a ticket. A pitch deck answers it in twenty minutes for someone holding a cheque. A launch film answers it in ninety seconds for a person who came for something else. Same question, different patience.

That is why the formats look varied and the practice is narrow. I have built the story and the thing that carries it often enough to know which format a message deserves, and I arrive with a method rather than a house style to impose on you.

The description lag

Capability now arrives years before anyone can say plainly what it is for.

There is a lag between the moment a technology can do something and the moment anybody can say clearly what that something is, and the interesting thing about the present moment is that the lag has grown rather than shrunk. You would expect the opposite. We have more writers than ever pointed at this material, more explainers, more newsletters, more conference talks, and the volume of description is enormous. What has not kept pace is description that a person outside the field can act on, and the distance between those two quantities is where most of the confusion in this industry currently lives.

Consider what actually happened in the last week of January 2025. A Chinese lab released a model that performed near the frontier at a training cost it claimed was a fraction of what American labs were spending, and within two days Nvidia lost something close to five hundred and ninety billion dollars of market capitalisation in a single session, the largest one-day loss any company has ever recorded. The technical fact was narrow and mostly about efficiency. The story that moved was much larger and mostly about whether the entire capital structure of the industry rested on an assumption that had just been falsified. Both of these cannot be right, and the gap between them was not filled by better information, because better information existed the whole time, in the paper, available to anyone. The gap was filled by the only description that was ready to hand.

This is what I mean by the description lag. It is not that nobody knew. It is that the available sentences were all built for a different question, and when a new question arrived the old sentences went to work on it anyway.

You can see the same thing at a smaller scale every time a lab ships. A model is released, the benchmark scores go up, and the field’s vocabulary offers two moves: the thing is more capable, or the benchmark is contaminated. Neither of those tells a person running a legal team or a hospital or a school anything they can use on Monday, because what they need to know is narrower and more awkward. Does it still fail on the thing it used to fail on. Does it fail in the same shape, so that the checks we built still catch it, or in a new shape that gets past them. What changed about the cost of running it at our volume. Those questions have no ready sentences, so mostly they go unasked in public and get answered privately and badly.

I think the reason for the lag is structural rather than a failure of effort. Description is expensive in a specific way: it requires that somebody hold the technical detail and the reader’s situation in mind at the same time, and almost nobody is paid to do both. The people who understand the detail are paid to advance it, and the incentive on their writing is to establish priority and defend a claim against peers. The people who understand the reader are paid to acquire attention, and the incentive on their writing is a headline that survives a scroll. These two groups produce enormous quantities of prose and almost none of it is aimed at the place where the two overlap, which is where a normal competent adult decides whether to change what they do.

What fills the vacuum is analogy, and analogy is where the real damage happens, because an analogy is a compressed argument that the reader accepts without noticing they have accepted anything. When a system is called a copilot, a whole set of conclusions arrives with the word: that there is a pilot, that the pilot is in command, that the copilot is qualified, that the plane has a destination somebody chose. When the same system is called a stochastic parrot, a different set arrives, equally uninspected. Neither phrase was chosen by anyone who thought carefully about which conclusions it would smuggle. They were chosen because they were vivid, and vividness is the trait that survives transmission.

The practical question is what closes the lag, and I have come to think the answer is unglamorous: someone has to write the sentence that names the failure. Capability is easy to describe because it has a shape everyone recognises, the shape of a thing that used to be impossible and now happens. Limits have no such shape. They are conditional, they come with an “unless,” they require holding two situations in mind, and they lose to the capability sentence every time they are put in the same room with it. So the limit has to be written into the capability sentence itself, in the same breath, where it cannot be separated out and discarded on the way to a headline. Not a limitations section, which is a place readers have already agreed to skip, but a clause inside the claim.

This model matches expert annotators on English clinical notes and performs at chance on the same task in Hindi. One sentence, and a person who reads it cannot go away with only the good half.

That kind of writing is harder than it looks, and most of the difficulty is social rather than intellectual. The person who writes it is volunteering information that costs them something, in a room full of people who are not volunteering theirs, and the immediate reward is that their result sounds smaller. The delayed reward is that they become the source people check against, which is the only durable position in a field that ships something new every eleven weeks.

The lag will close eventually, the way it closed for electricity and for the internet, when a generation grows up with the vocabulary already settled and stops noticing that anybody chose it. What is worth attending to now is who does the choosing, because those choices are being made this year, mostly by accident, mostly by people optimising for something else.

A benchmark is a sentence about what matters

Every measurement is an argument wearing the clothes of a fact.

A benchmark looks like a thermometer and behaves like an editorial. It appears to report a property of the world, the way a thermometer reports the temperature of a room, and because the output is a number it inherits the social privileges of numbers, which are considerable: a number can be cited without being defended, repeated without being understood, and placed in a slide where it will be believed by people who would have argued with the same claim written as a sentence. What the number actually contains is a long series of choices about what counts, and every one of those choices is contestable, and almost none of them are visible by the time the figure reaches a reader.

The choices start earlier than people think. Somebody decided which tasks belong in the set, which means somebody decided which competencies are the competencies. Somebody decided how to score partial credit, which sets the exchange rate between being confidently wrong and being usefully uncertain. Somebody decided what a passing grade looks like, and in doing so decided how good is good enough, which is a question about deployment and risk that has been quietly relocated into a spreadsheet. Somebody decided which languages the test runs in, and that decision alone determines whether a model that is excellent in English and broken in Marathi reads as excellent or as broken. None of this is hidden. All of it is in the papers. It simply does not survive the trip to the place where decisions get made.

April 2025 gave the field a clean demonstration of what happens when the argument underneath a number becomes visible. Meta put a version of Llama 4 on the LMArena leaderboard, it placed extremely well, and it then emerged that the version tested was an experimental build tuned for conversational appeal rather than the one shipped to developers. The company’s position was that it was permitted, and in the narrow sense of the rules it largely was. The reaction was not about rule-breaking. It was that everyone could suddenly see the joint: that a leaderboard reflecting human preference between two chat responses measures a specific thing, that the specific thing can be optimised for directly, and that optimising for it is not the same as the model being better at anything a person needs done. The number had always been an argument. For a week it was legible as one.

The uncomfortable part is that the fix people usually propose makes it worse. The proposal is always more benchmarks, broader coverage, a suite rather than a single figure, and this sounds like rigour while functioning as camouflage. Twelve numbers do not encode twelve arguments a reader can inspect; they encode one impression, which is that the thing was thoroughly tested, and impressions are harder to argue with than claims. A single number invites the question of what it measures. A dashboard of numbers repels that question by seeming to have answered it already.

What would actually help is naming, on the page, the thing the benchmark thinks is important, in a sentence with a subject and a verb. This set rewards fluent, well-structured answers to questions that have a known answer. This set rewards refusing when the answer is unknown. This set rewards being right about the small number of cases where being wrong is expensive, and treats everything else as noise. Written that way, the benchmark becomes checkable by a person who has never read the paper, because they can compare that sentence against the thing they actually need and see the mismatch. Written as a score, it cannot be checked at all, only trusted or dismissed.

There is a version of this argument that treats benchmark authors as villains, and that version is wrong and also uninteresting. The people building evaluations are doing the most useful work available to anybody who wants this technology to be accountable, and they are doing it with fewer resources and less glory than the people building models. The problem is not their motives. The problem is that a number travels and a caveat does not, and the entire chain from the paper to the press release to the procurement meeting is a series of compressions, each one lossy, each one dropping the qualifier first because the qualifier is the part that makes the sentence longer.

So the useful test for any benchmark is not whether it is rigorous. It is whether it can be run against you. A measurement that only ever gets reported when it flatters the reporter is not a measurement, it is a marketing asset with a methodology section. You can tell the difference by watching what happens in the second quarter. If competitors adopt the benchmark and publish their own numbers on it, including the bad ones, it was measuring something real and the field has agreed on it. Regulators know this, which is why modern technology law leans so heavily on process obligations rather than substantive ones. Maintain a risk management system. Keep technical documentation. It is a genuinely clever solution and it has a genuinely serious failure mode, which is that process obligations are satisfiable by the production of documents, and the production of documents is a competence that large firms have in abundance and small ones do not. A rule designed to be future-proof turns out to be, in practice, a rule that advantages whoever has the biggest compliance department.

A risk management system is a folder unless somebody has written, in words an engineer can act on, what counts as a risk in this product and what to do when one is found. It cannot require that the folder contain a sentence anybody can use, and whether it does is determined by a person with no legal title whose name appears nowhere in the file.

The obligations that bit early, the prohibitions and the transparency duties, produced observable behaviour almost immediately. The obligations three years out produced consultants. That is not cynicism about firms, it is how any organisation allocates attention under uncertainty: a rule that might change before it applies is rationally deprioritised, and announcing a long runway announces that it might change.

Which suggests that the most consequential choice in drafting is not the stringency of the rule but the distance to the date, and that a modest obligation arriving next year will alter more behaviour than a demanding one arriving in four. The third thing, and the one I find hardest, is that a deferral is a communication event whether or not anybody intends it as one. The technical content of the European decision was narrow. The story that travelled was that Europe blinked, and that story will now shape what firms in other jurisdictions expect when their own deadlines approach, which changes their behaviour today, in every market, regardless of what any regulator actually decides later. This is a real cost, it was probably not priced into the decision, and it is the kind of cost that people who write law for a living are not trained to see, because their training is in the text and the cost lives entirely in the reception.

It is the text, plus the schedule, plus what the schedule signals, plus the quality of the writing inside the documents the text requires, and the last of those is the part nobody is accountable for and the part that determines whether any of it reaches the engineer.

Say the hard thing first

The fastest route to being believed is to volunteer the information that costs you.

A reader arrives with an objection already loaded. There are two places that objection can be dealt with: in your second paragraph, where you can shape it, or silently in their head for the remainder of the page, while they decide what you are hiding. Most institutional writing chooses the second by default, not out of dishonesty but because the objection is uncomfortable, the schedule is tight, and nobody in the review wants to be the person who added the paragraph about what the thing does badly.

The move that works is to put the objection in the opening. Stated plainly, early, in the reader’s own words, without softening it into a strength. This requires review before anything reaches a client, and if you need output you can ship unread, this is the wrong tool. Two sentences of that kind cost you the readers who needed unreviewed output, who were going to churn in month two and write something unpleasant on the way out. Everybody else now reads the rest of the page with their guard down, because you have demonstrated the one thing that matters at the top of any funnel, which is that you will tell them the bad part.

The reason this works is structural rather than moral, and being precise about that matters, because it is usually taught as honesty and honesty is not the mechanism. A reader evaluating a claim is running a model of the writer, and that model has one main variable: will this person volunteer information that costs them. Every hedge, every omission, every superlative without a referent updates the model downward. A stated limitation updates it sharply upward, and the update transfers to every other claim on the page, including the ones you have offered no evidence for. That transfer is the whole return. You spend one paragraph and you buy credibility on all the paragraphs you cannot prove.

It also does something inside the organisation, which I think is the larger effect. To write the objection first you have to know what it is, and a surprising number of teams have yet to write it down. Ask four people what the strongest argument against buying their product is and you will frequently get four answers, none of which match what the sales team hears on calls. That gap is a strategy problem wearing a copywriting costume, and the writing is simply where it surfaces first.

There are ways to do this badly and they are common enough to name.

The fake objection, where the stated weakness is a strength in costume. We may be too thorough for teams who want something quick. Readers have seen this in interviews their entire working lives and it registers as evasion with extra steps, which is worse than saying nothing, because it demonstrates that you know the move and chose to fake it.

The buried objection, technically present and structurally absent: paragraph fourteen, shorter sentences, a heading that says Limitations, positioned where a skimmer rarely reaches. The information is there and does no work, because the work depends entirely on the reader meeting it before they have decided.

The symmetrical objection, where every drawback is immediately balanced by a compensating benefit in the same sentence. While the model is slower, it is considerably more accurate. This reads as a negotiation rather than a disclosure, and a reader who senses negotiation goes back to running the model of you.

The version that works has none of those tells. It states the cost, it does not immediately repair it, and it trusts the reader to weigh it. That trust is the actual content of the gesture: you are telling them you think they can handle a real trade-off, which is a form of respect that almost nothing else in commercial writing offers, and which people notice precisely because it is rare.

The hardest part is institutional rather than literary. Somebody has to approve it, and the person approving has spent a career learning that volunteering weakness is how you lose a deal, and they are not stupid, because in a pitch with three vendors and a scoring rubric they may well be right. The argument for doing it anyway is about which readers you are trying to keep. Objection-first writing loses the reader who was choosing on a checklist and wins the reader who was choosing on judgment. If your product is genuinely better for a narrower group, that trade is obviously correct. If it is not, the writing has surfaced a problem that no amount of rearranging was going to solve.

What a caveat costs

Every hedge is a second of somebody's attention, and the reader is paying for it.

A hedge in a sentence looks free. It occupies a few words, it makes the claim more defensible, and the writer who added it feels more honest than they did a minute earlier. What it actually does is spend the reader’s attention, which is fixed, and dilute the claim, which was the only reason they were reading. The cost is real and it is paid in a currency nobody wrote on the page.

The useful move is to separate hedges into two kinds, because once you can see the difference you cannot unsee it.

An informing hedge changes what the reader will do. This holds for English and degrades sharply on other scripts. A person who reads that now behaves differently: they will check something, or scope something, or stop. The words earned their seconds.

An insuring hedge changes what can be said about the writer later. Results may vary depending on a range of factors. Nobody’s afternoon changes. It exists so that a future person cannot accuse the author of having overclaimed, which means it is an insurance premium, paid by the reader, for the writer’s benefit. In most institutional documents it is the majority of the hedging by volume.

Once you start counting, the proportions are startling. Whole paragraphs of insurance in system cards, in launch posts, in strategy decks, each clause individually reasonable, each one added by a careful person for a defensible reason, cumulatively a wall of premium payments funded by the attention you needed for the actual claim. The document ends up honest about everything and useful about nothing, which is a real failure state and one that no review process is designed to catch, because every review process adds hedges and none of them removes any.

The test is to ask out loud what the hedge is protecting against and listen to the answer. If the answer is that a reader might do the wrong thing, keep it, and move it into the claim sentence where it cannot be skipped. If the answer is that somebody might quote this back at you, you have just found four seconds to spend somewhere better.

Placement matters at least as much as presence, and this is where most documents go wrong even when their hedging is honest. Information that arrives after the reader has formed a judgment is received as administration. They have already updated; the update has already propagated to their colleagues; the qualifier arrives into a mind that has closed. This is why a limitations section at the end of a document is, structurally, a place to put things you have decided the reader will skip. If the qualification matters, it goes in the sentence with the claim, same breath, same clause, no escape route. If it does not matter enough to survive that, it did not matter.

The third move is the one I resisted longest and now believe is the most valuable. Sometimes the correct edit is to make a smaller claim rather than a hedged one. A big claim wrapped in qualifiers is a worse object than a small claim stated flat, because the reader has to perform the subtraction themselves and will perform it badly, generally in the direction least flattering to you. This works reliably for one narrow case is stronger than this may work broadly under certain conditions, even though the second sounds more ambitious, because the narrow claim is checkable and the broad one is weather.

There is an argument that all of this is a luxury available only to people who are not being sued, and I take it seriously, because legal review is not a stylistic preference and counsel is usually right about the specific risk they are naming. But a great deal of what gets attributed to legal review was never required by anybody in legal. It was added pre-emptively by a writer imagining what counsel might say, which is a different and much more expansive standard, and it accounts for more damage than any actual lawyer has ever done to a document. The fix is to ask, not to guess, and the answer is frequently that the clause can go.

What makes this worth caring about beyond craft is that the distribution of hedges is not random. The claims that get hedged most heavily are the ones about harm, because those are the ones with legal exposure, which means the qualifying language accumulates precisely where the reader most needs a clean sentence. A field that hedges its capability claims lightly and its harm claims heavily has, without intending anything, built a machine that transmits good news cleanly and bad news at a loss. Nobody designed that. It is the emergent result of thousands of small individually reasonable decisions about where to add four words.

Trust accrues slowly and collapses at once

What an institution may say in month one, and what it has to earn the right to say by month six.

Trust accrues in a straight line and collapses in a step, and almost every communication problem worth solving comes back to that asymmetry. An institution earns belief slowly, a claim at a time, each one checked against experience. Then one claim fails to survive contact and the entire accumulated stock goes at once, including the parts that were true. The arithmetic is brutal and it is the only arithmetic in this field that reliably keeps behaving.

Which means the useful question is rarely whether a claim is accurate. It is whether the claim is available yet.

There is a sequence, and organisations break it constantly, usually out of impatience rather than dishonesty.

In the first month you may describe what the thing is. What it does, who it is for, what it replaces. That is the whole permitted range, and it is far larger than it feels, because most organisations are so poor at describing what the thing is that doing it plainly is already a differentiator. The claims available at this stage are structural, and nobody has to trust you to verify any of them, which is exactly why you are allowed to make them.

By month three you may describe what it has done. Named users, specific outcomes, a number with a method attached. The shift is from category to evidence, and evidence requires that somebody outside the building agreed to be cited. The impatient version of this stage is a metric with no denominator, and a reader with any experience reads a bare metric as a confession.

Around month six you may describe what it means. Claims about the category, about where things are going, about why the old way is ending. These are the sentences founders and ministers want to open with in week one, and they are the most expensive sentences in the language, because they can only be underwritten by the two earlier stages. An institution that makes a meaning claim before it has made an evidence claim has told its reader that it prefers the sound of the sentence to the work.

The sequence holds because each stage is verifiable at the price the reader is currently willing to pay. Month one, free. Month three, a click. Month six, a judgment. Sell a judgment to somebody who has paid nothing yet and they decline, and that decline is permanent in a way the first two are not.

Then there is the collapse side, which is the part almost nobody plans for and which is where the real positioning lives.

Every product and every institution has a moment where a person tries to undo the relationship. The cancellation, the export, the deletion, the refund, the appeal against a decision, the request to have something taken down. Organisations invest heavily in the front door and staff the exit with a form. This is a mistake of enormous magnitude and it is made almost universally, because the exit is measured as a cost centre and its actual function is as the single largest determinant of what people say about you when you are not in the room. A person who leaves easily says so, and the saying compounds in your favour for years. A person who has to fight their way out becomes a permanent negative reference, and they are believed instantly, because everybody listening has been trapped by something.

The practical rule that follows is to write the exit before the entrance. If the sentence describing how somebody stops using this is embarrassing, that sentence is your real positioning and everything in the deck is decoration.

One more thing about the collapse, because recovery is possible and looks nothing like the genre that has grown up around it. What repairs a room is short, specific, offers no reasons, and is followed immediately by continuing to do the work. The length of an explanation is read as the size of the guilt. Organisations that survive a bad month are the ones that say the true small thing and go back to shipping; the ones that publish twelve hundred words of context are still explaining a year later, because a long explanation invites a long argument and the argument is now the story.

Slow up, fast down. Say only what can be checked at the price the reader is currently willing to pay. And build the exit properly, because that is the room where you find out what you actually earned.

The format assumes the reader arrived with a question. Mostly they arrive with an attitude.

The explainer assumes a reader who has a question and is waiting to have it answered. Occasionally that reader exists. Far more often the person in front of you arrived with a position, acquired secondhand, held loosely but held, and what they are doing while they read is not learning but checking whether you are on their side.

This is why explaining to an occupied reader mostly fails, and fails in a specific and recognisable way: they read the whole thing, follow every sentence, and leave holding exactly the belief they came in with. The information went in. The belief did not move, because the explainer never touched it. It answered a question they had stopped asking some time ago.

The mechanism is not mysterious. A belief about a technology is rarely a belief about the technology. It is a belief about the people associated with it, about what they are likely to do with it, and about whether the person holding the belief will end up on the good or bad end of that. An explainer addresses the technology and leaves the rest untouched, so the reader absorbs the mechanism and re-attaches it to the same social judgment they started with, now better informed and equally unmoved.

You can watch this play out with any widely misunderstood system. Explain thoroughly how a recommendation algorithm ranks items, and a reader who believes the platform is manipulating them will finish your piece believing that the manipulation is more sophisticated than they had realised. Nothing you wrote was wrong. You simply supplied better evidence for the position they already had, which is what any competent mind does with new information about a thing it has already judged.

The format that does move somebody is older and less comfortable, and it starts by stating their position back to them more precisely than they had stated it themselves. Not a caricature to knock down, which everybody can see coming, but the strongest available version, the one they would have written if they had spent longer on it. This does two things at once. It demonstrates that you understood, which is the only thing that buys permission to say anything afterwards. And it frequently surfaces, to the reader, that their position was resting on a specific claim they had not examined, because until you wrote it down clearly they had never quite seen its shape.

Then, and only then, you introduce the thing that does not fit. One thing, not five. An explainer wants comprehensiveness; the job here wants a single well-chosen fact that their position cannot absorb without adjustment. A reader who has to do that adjustment themselves has changed their mind in the only way anybody ever does, which is privately and with a sense of having worked it out.

The reason the explainer format persists despite not working is that it is pleasant to produce and pleasant to receive. Writing one feels like teaching. Reading one feels like learning. Both feelings are real and neither is evidence that anything happened. Whereas the format that works is unpleasant at both ends: the writer has to spend a third of the piece making the other side’s argument, which is uncomfortable and looks like weakness to a colleague reading the draft, and the reader has to sit through a passage that feels like it is going the wrong way.

There is an organisational reason too, which is that explainers are safe. Nobody has ever been reprimanded for a clear, accurate, thorough explanation of how something works. Stating the opposing position well, in a document published under an institution’s name, can absolutely get somebody reprimanded, because it looks from a distance like agreement, and the distance is where the reprimand comes from.

None of this is an argument against explanation as such. A person who genuinely has a question deserves a clear answer and the world needs far more of them than it has. It is an argument against reaching for explanation as a default when the actual problem is that somebody has already decided. Those two situations require different writing, they are easy to distinguish if you look, and the tell is simple: an audience with a question asks how, and an audience with a position asks whether you would say that.

Find out what they think first. Then decide what you are writing.

That is a hierarchy decision made under a hard constraint, and it is more disciplined than what most websites do with unlimited space.

The moment the painter’s name is attached to the letterform, the relationship changes from collection to citation, and citation is the difference between an archive and an extraction. Flex printing also removed a constraint, and removing the constraint destroyed the discipline. A printed board can hold as much text as you like at whatever size you like, so it does, and the result is the visual noise of any Indian commercial street now: boards carrying nine pieces of information, each one legible only if you stop, which nobody does. The hand-painted board was better not because the painter had more taste but because his medium charged him for every letter, and a medium that charges you for every letter produces editing.

That is the transferable idea and it generalises well past signage. Constraint produces hierarchy. Every long launch post and every crowded landing page is a flex board.

The painters also understood something about permanence that is almost entirely absent from digital practice. Fifteen years on a wall means you cannot write anything you will want to take back, which rules out most of what passes for positioning, because most positioning is written to be true for a quarter. A claim built to survive fifteen years of the shopkeeper’s actual behaviour has to be narrow and it has to be about what the shop actually does, and that pressure produces sentences of a kind that no campaign cycle will ever produce.

The last thing, and the one worth sitting with: the painter accepted that the reader owed him nothing. The board had to work on somebody who was going somewhere else, who had not asked, and who would give it about a second and a half. Most writing today is produced as though the reader has already decided to care, which is the one thing a board can never assume and the one thing a landing page assumes constantly.

A team assessing whether a system is working reaches for the measures that exist, and the measures that exist were built against English tasks with English assumptions about what a task even is. The result is a quiet and almost invisible bias in what counts as working, one that no individual decision produced and that nobody is in a position to correct, because correcting it would require a vocabulary that has not been built.

None of this is an argument for linguistic purism, which is generally a waste of everybody’s time and frequently a cover for something worse. English is the working language of the field and will remain so. The argument is narrower: that a field whose entire conceptual vocabulary originates in one place has one set of instincts, that it will experience those instincts as common sense rather than as a position, and that the first people to notice this are usually the ones working in a second language, because they can feel the seam.

Put that way, the honest answer is that they should not, unless the designation is actually delivering them something, and for large stretches of the last decade it has not been obvious that it was.

UNESCO has since raised concerns about conservation lapses, which triggered the predictable cycle: a flurry of committee activity, a set of commitments, and coverage framed around the risk of losing the status. That framing is revealing, because it treats the title as the asset. The title is not the asset. The title is a claim about the asset, and a city that spends its energy protecting the claim rather than the thing has quietly inverted the relationship, which is the single most common failure in positioning of any kind, at any scale.

What would a serious version look like. It would start from the observation that a living heritage district survives only if living there is better than the alternative, which is a statement about services and money rather than about aesthetics. It would make the trade explicit and legible to residents: here is what you give up, here is what you get, here is who to call when the roof goes. And it would treat the residents as the primary audience for all of its communication, rather than as a constituency to be managed while the real communication is aimed at tourists and committees.

That last point is where most heritage programmes go wrong and it generalises well beyond cities. When a project has one audience that can end it and another audience that makes it look good, almost all of the writing ends up aimed at the second, because the second is the one that produces visible wins. Brochures, exhibitions, festivals, a well-designed website in English. Meanwhile the people who actually determine whether the thing survives receive a notice in the mail about a permit process.

There is a version of this that is working, and it is worth being specific about what it consists of, because the successful examples are never about better storytelling. They are about somebody making the maintenance economics work: a revolving fund, a trained local workforce, a simplified approval that takes weeks instead of years. Once those exist, the storytelling becomes almost trivially easy, because the story is true and the people in it will tell it themselves. Before they exist, no amount of communication helps, and communication deployed in their absence is correctly read as public relations.

The thing I would want anybody thinking about this to sit with is that the 2017 designation was, in narrative terms, an enormous success and, in operational terms, a starting gun that a lot of people heard as a finish line. That confusion is very common and it is the specific hazard of winning an argument in public. A claim accepted feels like a problem solved. It is the opposite: an accepted claim is a debt, denominated in everything you now have to do to keep it true, and the interest comes due in a decade when somebody checks.

What a model does to a script it cannot read

Ask for a shop sign in English and you get typography. Ask in Gujarati and you get a guess.

Ask an image model for a shop sign in English and it will hand you dozens of typographic registers: a condensed grotesque, a slab, a script with a competent connecting stroke, letterforms that look like they were drawn by somebody who had seen letterforms. Ask for the same sign in Gujarati or Tamil or Odia and what comes back is broken. The letters are approximately the right shape and wrong in the details that matter, the conjuncts collapse, the matras attach at angles no hand would produce, and the range of styles narrows to one, which is a sort of generic institutional look with no history behind it.

Both of these outputs are reported by most evaluations as the same capability: the model can render text in images. That equivalence is the interesting part, because it is not an oversight by careless people. It follows directly from who writes evaluations and which languages they can read. A grader who does not read Gujarati cannot distinguish a good Gujarati letterform from a plausible one, so the benchmark measures whether text appeared, and text appeared.

This is starting to be addressed, and the work is coming from where you would expect. AI4Bharat has been building Indic datasets and evaluations for years, BharatGen has been releasing open datasets for Indian language AI, and there is now a small but growing set of benchmarks specifically constructed for low-resource Indian languages rather than translated from English ones. The distinction between constructed and translated is the whole thing. A translated benchmark asks whether the model can do an English task in another language, which measures translation. A constructed one asks whether it can do the task that actually arises in that language, which is a different and much harder question, and it is the only one that tells you anything about deployment.

For scripts, the gap is sharper than for text generally, because typography encodes cultural information at a level below meaning. A Gujarati signboard painted in Amdavad in 1970 and one painted in 1995 are different objects to anybody who grew up reading them, in the way that a Victorian shopfront and a 1980s one are different to an English eye. That difference is legible to millions of people and invisible to every current evaluation, which means a model can be shipped as fully capable in a language while producing letterforms that read, to native speakers, as uncanny.

The reason this deserves more attention than it gets is that it is a clean case of a general problem. Evaluation determines what counts as done. When the people who build evaluations and the people who will live with the system are different populations, the system will be declared finished at the point where it satisfies the first group. Everything downstream inherits that judgment: procurement decisions, product roadmaps, the allocation of engineering time. No individual acted badly. The measurement simply had a blind spot the shape of an entire readership.

Fixing it is less exotic than it sounds. You need a corpus of real letterforms with provenance, which in most Indian languages means going out and recording what is actually painted on walls, with the painter’s name and the year, because the alternative corpus, digital fonts, is small and homogeneous and does not represent how the script is actually used. You need graders who read the script and are asked specific questions rather than general ones: is this letterform correct, is this conjunct formed properly, would a reader find this style plausible for this kind of business. And you need a rubric written tightly enough that two graders agree, which in practice takes several rewrites, because every ambiguity in the wording comes back as noise in the results.

That last part is where these projects usually die, and it is worth saying plainly that the bottleneck is writing rather than modelling. Anybody can collect images. The difficulty is specifying, in language that survives being read by thirty different people, what you want them to look at and what to do when the letterform is legible and wrong at the same time. A vague rubric produces data that looks like a benchmark and measures agreement rather than quality.

There is a decent argument that all of this will be solved incidentally as models improve, and it may be. But incidental solutions arrive in an order determined by who complains, and a capability gap that nobody can see will not generate complaints. The scripts that get fixed first will be the ones with a benchmark, because a benchmark is the mechanism by which a problem becomes visible to people who cannot perceive it directly. Everything else stays in the category of things that work fine, according to the only people who were asked.

A place has to be described before it is believed

GIFT City is a test of whether a jurisdiction can be positioned the way a product is.

A financial centre is one of the few things in the world that is made almost entirely out of belief. The buildings matter and the fibre matters and the tax treatment matters a great deal, but none of them produces a financial centre by themselves, because what a financial centre actually consists of is a large number of people who each believe that the other people will be there next year. That is a coordination problem, and coordination problems are solved by description: everybody needs a shared account of what the place is, or nobody moves first.

GIFT City, on the Sabarmati between Ahmedabad and Gandhinagar, is the most interesting current test of whether a jurisdiction can be built this way deliberately. It has the regulatory apparatus, a dedicated authority, an exchange, a growing population of funds and global capability centres, and a policy environment that keeps adding reasons to be there. What it has been working on for a decade, with genuinely mixed results, is the description.

The difficulty is structural rather than promotional. A new centre has to be described in terms of the old ones, because that is the only vocabulary its audience has, and every available comparison imports a set of expectations it cannot yet meet. Call it India’s Singapore and you have invited a comparison on depth of talent pool and decades of institutional history. Call it a tax jurisdiction and you have positioned it as arbitrage, which attracts exactly the capital that leaves first. Call it a gateway, which is the term that has mostly stuck, and you have said something true and almost contentless, because a gateway is defined by what passes through it rather than by anything it is.

This is the same problem any new category faces and it has one reliable solution, which is to describe the specific thing you are better at rather than the general thing you resemble. Not a gateway, but the place where a particular kind of transaction that used to require three jurisdictions now requires one. That sentence is checkable. A person can hold it up against their own situation and see whether it applies, and the ones for whom it applies will move, and their moving is the evidence that makes the next sentence possible.

The temptation in the other direction is enormous and almost irresistible for any government project, because the audience for the communication is frequently not the market but the political centre, and the political centre rewards scale language: number of entities registered, square feet, projected employment. Those figures are real and they are the wrong instrument, because a fund manager deciding where to domicile is not asking how big the place is. She is asking whether the thing she needs to do is easier here than where she is now, and how quickly she can find out, and what happens when something goes wrong at eleven at night.

That last question is the one that decides these things and it is the one nobody writes about. The reputation of any financial jurisdiction is made almost entirely out of stories about failure handling: how a dispute went, how long an approval actually took when it was complicated rather than standard, whether the regulator picked up the phone. Those stories circulate privately, they are extremely durable, and no amount of published material can override them. Which means the communication that matters most is not communication at all. It is the operational behaviour that generates the stories, and the only useful thing a communications function can do is make sure the good stories are findable by the people who are currently asking around.

There is a version of this argument that says places cannot be positioned, that they simply grow or fail to, and I think that is too fatalistic. Places do get positioned, and the successful ones tend to have done a particular thing: they found the narrow use case where they were unambiguously better, said it plainly for a decade without getting bored, and let the broader claim arrive later, from other people. Dublin did that with fund administration. Luxembourg did it with cross-border vehicles. Neither started by announcing that it was a global hub, and both ended up being described that way by others, which is the only version of that sentence worth having.

The general lesson is unglamorous and applies far outside finance. You do not get to make the meaning claim first. You get to make the structural claim, then the evidence claim, and only then does somebody else make the meaning claim on your behalf. A place that describes itself as a global hub before anybody else has said so is doing the sequence backwards, and the audience can hear it, because the sequence is audible.

The corpus is the constitution

What a model can be asked to imagine is decided years before the prompt.

The obvious way to describe a large model’s failure is to say it does not have enough data. Ask an image model for a shop sign in Hindi and the letterforms come back broken. Ask for the same sign in English and you get ten typographies, each of them competent. The gap is real and it is easy to measure. The temptation is to treat the fix as arithmetic: pour more of the missing script into the training set and the gap closes. This is true and it is insufficient, and the reason it is insufficient is the interesting part.

A training corpus is not an ingredient list. It is a legislature that has already voted, and by the time a model reaches you the votes are on things you cannot see. Which words appear next to which. Which sentences appear in the same document. Which images the captions treated as worth captioning. Which categories the raters recognised. Every one of those decisions is a small constitutional act, and by the time the training run finishes you have a system whose imagination is bounded by the reading habits of the people who assembled its diet. The word bias suggests a wrong opinion laid over a correct machine. What is happening here is different. It is a prior about what exists.

The clearest place to see this is in scripts the corpus barely met. A model trained mostly on English does not merely know less about Gujarati; it knows a different Gujarati. It has met the language through translations, through captions of Wikipedia images, through the small share of Indian websites that were content-farmed into English SEO before anyone crawled them. What it has not met is the actual daily surface of the script: shop boards, wedding invitations, movie posters, courtroom forms, the register a mother uses to text her son, the register the son uses back. Ask it to make a shop sign and it produces something that looks like an attempt to remember a script rather than an attempt to use one, because the training taught it that this script mostly appeared as an object to be described, not as a thing to be written.

This has practical consequences that get missed because they hide inside a familiar frame. The frame is: the model is worse at Hindi than at English, so we should train more Hindi. The consequence hiding inside is: the model has inherited a set of readers whose interests decided which Hindi mattered. Those readers were disproportionately literary, disproportionately governmental, and disproportionately based in Delhi. A company deploying a chatbot into Ahmedabad, or Coimbatore, or Guwahati is not deploying a model that knows less about its market. It is deploying a model whose imagination of that market was formed by people who never went. Adding data helps only if the data is chosen by somebody who has been.

Which brings me to the second act of this argument, which is about who chooses. In a field this young, most corpora were assembled by whichever team could scrape fastest, filtered by whatever quality signals the labellers could agree on, and rated by workers paid by the hour to answer questions written in English. There was no meeting where anyone said this document is worth keeping and this one is not; there was a pipeline, and the pipeline had defaults, and the defaults were English. This is the ordinary way large systems get built and it works fine in domains where the default reader is the marginal reader. It stops working the moment you take the system across the language boundary. The pipeline still runs, the metrics still look reasonable, and the model behaves in a way the numbers do not predict, because the numbers were built by the same defaults that built the corpus.

For a person deploying one of these models into a real market, this suggests a working rule more useful than the debate about which lab is winning. The model is a jurisdiction. It will govern your product for as long as the product exists, and its constitution was written by people whose interests were not yours. That is not an argument against using it, any more than the fact that a bank is governed by American law is an argument for an Indian founder to avoid opening one. It is an argument for reading the constitution before signing the contract, and there is a small number of questions that get you most of the way there.

The first question is what the model was mostly trained on, by document type rather than by language. A model that read the internet is not a model that read your industry. A model that read scientific papers has different priors than one that read chat logs. The lab will not always tell you the answer; the model will, if you hand it a domain document and watch what it flatters and what it flattens.

The second question is who rated the outputs during fine-tuning. A model trained to please a graduate student in San Francisco is a different animal from a model trained to please a call-centre trainer in Manila, and both are useful, and neither is neutral. If you are building for a third audience you are asking the model to play a role it was not cast for, and you will pay for the mismatch in ways that show up as low retention rather than as anything a benchmark catches.

The third question is what happens to the model when it is asked to work in a script or a register the corpus barely met. This is the question that matters most for anybody selling into markets where English is a second language and where a model that reads English as its native tongue will produce copy that reads as slightly foreign for reasons neither the writer nor the reader can name. That foreignness compounds. Every sentence is a small failure of belonging, and the audience feels it before they can say what is wrong.

None of this is an argument for training your own model, which is a solution most companies cannot afford and few actually need. It is an argument for reading the constitution of the model you did buy, and for accepting that the reading is part of the work, not a preamble to it. The people who will build durable products on top of this generation of systems are the ones who understand which questions the systems were built to answer well, which questions they were built to answer at all, and which questions the systems were quietly excused from answering because nobody in the room spoke the language of the person who would eventually ask them.
